Abstract

In-hand reorientation enables robots to place an object in the target pose required by downstream tasks. Existing proprioceptive approaches have demonstrated goal-conditioned reorientation, but following continuous-valued commands precisely without external object-pose sensing remains challenging. We present DexOrient, a proprioception-only framework for precise, command-guided reorientation of objects in hand. An object property adapter (OPA) infers a latent representation of object properties, while a rotation progress estimator (RPE) predicts per-step angular increments that are accumulated into rotation progress. A policy trained in simulation uses both estimates to rotate the object and stabilize it at the commanded angle. The RPE is fine-tuned on real-robot trajectories collected from its own closed-loop rollouts, reducing the bias in tracking rotation progress.

On the LEAP Hand, DexOrient achieves 81.0% success within a 20° tolerance for commands from 22.5° to 180°, up from 57.5% before fine-tuning. In comparison, an RMA-style baseline that jointly encodes object properties and rotation progress in a single latent achieves a lower success rate of 44.0%. Qualitative demonstrations include unseen objects, mid-command mass changes, and external disturbances.

How DexOrient Works

1

Base policy with a privileged encoder

The base policy π is trained with PPO in Isaac Lab on a 7.5 cm cylinder. Its observation is the last three proprioceptive frames (16 joint positions + 16 joint targets each), the commanded angle θcmd, the rotation progress φt, and an 8-dimensional latent zt that a privileged encoder computes from object position, scale, mass, fingertip friction and centre-of-mass offset. Progress is deliberately kept out of the privileged vector. The reward rewards error reduction, proximity to the target, keeping the object at its canonical in-hand location, stillness once on target, and a success bonus after 50 held steps. Scale, mass, centre of mass, friction, and joint stiffness and damping are randomized.

2

Object property adapter (OPA)

The privileged latent is unavailable on hardware. The OPA is a stateless temporal convolutional network that infers ẑt from a window of the 30 most recent proprioceptive frames. Because object properties stay nearly constant within an episode, a bounded window carries enough evidence to identify them. With the policy and encoder frozen, the OPA is trained by MSE regression to the privileged latent.

3

Rotation progress estimator (RPE)

Progress accumulates from the command boundary and has no fixed-window observer, so the RPE, a GRU over the last 8 frames, predicts only the per-step yaw increment; an external integrator sums the increments from command onset. A drift-aware loss penalizes both the per-step error and its running sum over 60-step horizons. The RPE is trained on real-robot data labeled by the overhead camera: R0 on 400 camera-driven commands, then fine-tuned DAgger-style on 200 commands collected with the estimator itself in the loop, giving R1, the deployed estimator.

Evaluation protocol

  • Commands are sampled uniformly from [22.5°, 180°]. Simulation evaluates 8,192 commands with a 200-step budget; hardware evaluates 200 commands with a 150-step budget, at 20 Hz in both.
  • Achieved angle θach is the mean progress over the longest interval in the last 50 steps whose range stays within 20°; if that interval is shorter than 10 steps the trial has not settled and counts as a failure. A trial succeeds if |θach − θcmd| is within 10°, 15° or 20°. We also report the median terminal error and the slope, intercept and R² of a least-squares fit of achieved on commanded angle.
  • Ground truth on hardware comes from an overhead RealSense camera tracking a dark arrow on the object's top face. It labels estimator training data and scores trials; the deployed system never sees it.
  • Timer baseline. A HORA-style continuous-rotation policy that never observes the command, stopped by a quadratic time-to-angle map fitted on 236 hardware trials. It tests whether elapsed time alone can stand in for progress.

Where the remaining error comes from. With the camera supplying progress, the same base policy and OPA succeed on every command, so manipulation is not the bottleneck. Replacing the camera with the RPE gives 81.0% over 200 commands (slope 0.92, R² 0.89, median terminal error 8.3°). The gap is progress estimation.

Time is not a substitute for proprioception. The Timer baseline's error never converges: its median rises from about 15° after 20 control steps to over 40° near the end, because the continuous-rotation policy needs anywhere from 98 to 195 steps to turn the object by 180°. The RPE's error settles at about 10°.

On-policy fine-tuning removes a systematic bias. The initial estimator R0, trained on camera-driven rollouts, runs ahead of the camera for small commands and behind for large ones once it drives the policy itself (fitted slope 1.25, 57.5% success). One DAgger-style round on 200 self-collected commands lowers the early error peak from 14.0° to 9.4° and lifts success to 81.0%, roughly halving both the median terminal error and the mean estimation error. A second round reaches 85.0% in closed loop but does not improve the estimator itself and adds a positive offset, so R1 is deployed everywhere.

Explicit vs. Implicit Encoding of Rotation Progress

RMA-style distillation puts every unobserved quantity into one privileged vector and recovers all of them with a single adaptation module. Goal-conditioned reorientation needs rotation progress to decide when to stop, so the direct extension appends φt to the privileged state and asks one 8-dimensional latent to carry object properties and progress together (implicit). DexOrient instead keeps progress out of the latent and supplies it to the policy through a dedicated estimator (explicit). A separate base policy is trained for each encoding with the same PPO procedure; the implicit adapter uses the same GRU backbone as the RPE so that capacity is comparable.

Explicit DexOrient

privileged state pt
position, scale, mass, friction, CoM→encoder G / OPA (TConv, 30 frames)→object latent zt
proprio window (8 frames)→RPE (GRU) + Σ→progress φ̂t
proprio, θcmd, zt, φ̂t→base policy π→joint targets

The latent is reserved for object properties, which every contact re-evidences. Progress, a command-reset integral, is handled by a module built for integration.

Implicit RMA-style baseline

privileged state pt + progress φt→encoder GI→joint latent zIt
proprio window→GRU adapter must recover both→ẑIt
proprio, θcmd, ẑIt→base policy π→joint targets

One latent must hold a property that a bounded window can identify and a history-dependent quantity that no bounded window can observe.

Real-Robot Experiments

Command Following

One trial per commanded angle on a 16-DoF LEAP Hand, driven by proprioception only. The overlay is the overhead camera's arrow tracker, used for evaluation and never by the policy or the estimator.

Generalization and Robustness (qualitative)

The deployed system, unchanged, in three settings beyond the quantitative protocol: everyday objects in place of the training cylinder, a mass change during a command, and an external disturbance. In each case the hand completes the rotation and stabilizes the object without dropping it.
